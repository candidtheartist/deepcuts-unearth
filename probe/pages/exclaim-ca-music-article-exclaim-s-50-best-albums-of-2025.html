Exclaim!'s 50 Best Albums of 2025

BY Exclaim! StaffPublished Nov 27, 2025

Some years, the music world offers up clear consensus stand-outs, while others are an anything-goes mash of unique favourites. This year was decidedly the latter. In lieu of anything as dominant as Brat Summer, our favourite albums of 2025 include esoteric Canadiana and the return of rock as a source of innovation. A few familiar superstars have made their way into this year's mix, but, unlike last year's explosion of highbrow pop, 2025 was the year of the underdog.

See all of our year-end lists for 2025 here.

50. Addison Rae
Addison
(Columbia Records)

From Angelyne to Paris Hilton to Addison Rae, there's always been space for the influencer-turned-pop star. On her debut album, her Y2K synthpop is equally campy and contemplative, more of a confession than a call to the dance floor. Improbably artful, it's a testament to a girl-next-door's determination to turn daydreams into reality. "Fame is a gun and I point it blind," sings Rae, but it's clear she knows she's a sharpshooter.
Isabel Glasgow

49. Stereolab
Instant Holograms on Metal Film
(Warp Records / Duophonic UHF Disks)

A decade after the members of Stereolab decided to focus on other projects in 2009, the English-French avant-pop outfit reformed and started touring again while producing expanded reissues for most of their albums. They proved it was more than a typical nostalgia cash-in with Instant Holograms on Metal Film. Their 11th full-length showed that Tim Gane and Lætitia Sadier still have something essential to say about the state of the world, and that they are the only ones who could deliver these heavy sociopolitical messages in their peculiarly trippy, baroque-pop-exotica-tronica kind of way.
Alan Ranta

48. Great Grandpa
Patience, Moonbeam
(Run for Cover Records)

Having nearly disbanded before releasing their third studio album, as members moved into new stages of life and different parts of the world, Seattle-formed indie-emo/alt-country troupe Great Grandpa did eventually regroup for their follow-up to 2019's Four of Arrows, and it's their best record to date. They didn't exactly pick up where they left off; now wiser and primed for experimentation, the band deliver a cornucopia of sound on Patience, Moonbeam, with bandleader Al Menne's dynamic and HRT-fortified vocals backed by a collage of instruments including cello, banjo, clarinet and synths, flitting between folky holiness, distorted grungy breakdowns, and listing off contemporary references as casually as one would grocery items in the aisles of Trader Joe's.
Allie Gregory

47. Kaytranada
AIN'T NO DAMN WAY!
(RCA Records)

As Kaytranada's star has risen, he's made the most of his fame by turning his albums into parades of big-name guests, making grand collaborative statements with 2019's BUBBA and 2024's TIMELESS. But for the streamlined, AIN'T NO DAMN WAY!, he strips down to basics, delivering 35 minutes of playful, hooky dance grooves and no features at all (save for a TLC sample on the closing track). The whole thing absolutely breezes past, making it impossible to resist reaching for the repeat button the second it's done. Whether coming out of subwoofers in the club or busted earbuds on the bus, there ain't no damn way to have more fun than this in 2025.
Alex Hudson

46. Julianna Riolino
Echo in the Dust
(MoonWhistle Records)

We've seen Canadian rock 'n' roll's future, and its name is Julianna Riolino. Riolino has a history of taking to stages with such might, it's as if she's finally free of something that had prevented her from being herself. She's no less spirited on Echo in the Dust, but her theatricality is more dynamic, digging into thoughtfully written songs exploring interpersonal transitions, almost like they're scenes in a larger play that's a memorably compelling emotional roller coaster. 
Vish Khanna

45. Greet Death
Die in Love
(Deathwish Inc.)

Once at the forefront of doomgaze, the heavy branch of shoegaze's recent renaissance, Greet Death choose to re-imagine themselves on Die in Love, their first album in six years, by adding soft layers of optimism in songs about being miserable and depressed. There are pretty, tranquil moments from both co-leads Logan Gaval and Harper Boyhtari, as their light, floating voices and newfound hopefulness feel almost as seismic as their big, crushing guitars. Die in Love's delightful sonic pivots between sludgy walls of sound, hushed acoustic ballads and rich storytelling have set Greet Death apart from their peers.
Chris Gee

44. Saba and No ID
From the Private Collection of Saba and No ID
(ARTium Recordings / Pivot Gang)

For anyone who knows what's good in Chicago rap, Saba and No ID's full-length collaboration deserved celebration before its release in the spring. From the Private Collection of Saba and No ID is a no-skip, cross-generational collaboration that has both artists at their peaks. With sampling worthy of the greats, this is another classic under their belts. This collaboration's crown jewel? "Westside Pt. 4," with MFNMelo's career-best-to-date verse shining atop it. 
Nathan Chizen

43. They Are Gutting a Body of Water
LOTTO
(Julia's War Recordings / Smoking Room / ATO Records)

The genius of They Are Gutting a Body of Water (or TAGABOW) isn't new — LOTTO just helped others to realize it. The Philadelphia quartet have long been heralded in underground circles for their fusion of experimental rock and shoegaze, placing them among the leaders of the broader shoegaze revival. Propelled by Douglas Dulgarian's raw authenticity and self-awareness, TAGABOW have helped bring the genre's beloved distortion, reverb and gritty lo-fi into the future. Tracks like "the chase" and "american food" unspool like a stream-of-consciousness fever dream, one carefully curated cacophony after another. LOTTO is unrelenting in every sense, which feels a lot like life sometimes. Luckily we have TAGABOW to shepherd us through.
Dylan Barnabe

42. billy woods
GOLLIWOG
(Backwoodz Studioz)

You'd struggle to find a hip-hop record embodying a sociopolitical horror movie more than GOLLIWOG. billy woods, the phantom word tailor, weaves an unnerving tapestry of stories here: no verses or hooks to grab purchase, merely unhinged beats and a gruesome flow, grappling with fear and violence. Whether delivering crestfallen confessions or taking a blunt look into the history of Black America, GOLLIWOG shuffles between portentous and entrancing. You can get lost in these woods — just bring a lamp, because even the shadows carry knives in this realm.
Daryl Keating

41. Population II
Maintenant Jamais
(Bonsound)

Having already made a name for themselves with 2023's Électrons libres du Québec, Population II managed to surpass it on their third LP. While Maintenant Jamais feels punchier and more focused, it retains all the trio's ingredients: the experimentation, a perfect balance between spontaneity and prog excess, and a totally assumed québécitude. The whole album plays like a hallucinatory trip, with tracks merging into one another while providing unexpected twists and turns, full of wah-wah blasts and funky rhythms.
Bruno Coulombe

40. Model/Actriz
Pirouette
(True Panther Records)

Model/Actriz's sophomore effort is a raw, confessional album that explores queer joy and trauma in equal — and often simultaneous — measures. Its propulsive rhythms and clacking, grinding sounds are both blissed out and anxious, the band crushing and twirling. It's the sound of Grace Jones and Gaga, of Kylie fronting the Jesus Lizard, of Foucault necking with Ziggy. Nothing feels better than dancing with a purpose; Pirouette is a shimmering testament to movement as resistance, liberation and self-love.
Marko Djurdjić

39. The Armed
THE FUTURE IS HERE AND EVERYTHING NEEDS TO BE DESTROYED
(Sargent House)

The Armed have always been a forward-thinking hardcore band, but on THE FUTURE IS HERE AND EVERYTHING NEEDS TO BE DESTROYED, they prove that they are indeed the future of the genre. While the album has a few catchy moments, it's mostly an explosive outburst of vitriolic rage at the terrible dystopian situation we seem to be hurtling towards. When it finally comes time to dismantle things and start building toward something better, this album will be the perfect soundtrack to that demolition.
Jeremy Sheehy

38. Sammy Virji
Same Day Cleaning
(Capitol Records / Polydor Records)

The face of 2020s UK garage is one that wears a smile wider than the net Sammy Virji casts on Same Day Cleaning. The likes of Skepta, MJ Cole, salute, Flowdan, Chris Lake and many others lend their tongues to sit over its joyous grab bag of horns and vocal distortion, and the endless groove of its four-on-the-floor beats. It doesn't take itself too seriously, but it's sound enough in its vision to not mess around.
Sydney Brasil

37. SUMAC and Moor Mother
The Film
(Thrill Jockey)

At the risk of sounding trite, this is the perfect album for our times. On paper, the combination of SUMAC's and Moor Mother's poetry might somewhat comically point to a twisted version of rap rock. The album identifies and explodes the possibilities of that genre mash. It's incomprehensibly cacophonous at times and startlingly barren at others. Its lyricism is politically raw and uncompromising in its criticism of power structures and those that benefit from them. The Film is subtly groovy, achingly gruff, and manages to capture and condense our present turmoil into something undeniably potent.
Tom Piekarski

36. Lily Allen
West End Girl
(BMG)

Lily Allen doesn't mince words. On West End Girl — her first album in seven years — the British singer unpacks the seething details of the downfall of her marriage. The project came together in less than two weeks last December, as Allen's despairing stories about infidelity and insecurity unfolded in real time. Drawing thematic comparisons to Beyoncé's Lemonade, songs like "Tennis" and "Pussy Palace" pull listeners into the whirlwind of Allen's disillusionment. Across 14 songs, the album emphasizes a hyper-specific narrative that captivates — it's a callback to Allen's indie sleaze debut, released almost two decades ago. West End Girl showcases Allen's ability to take a tragedy and craft a comeback that confidently fits into today's cultural zeitgeist.
Heather Taylor-Singh

35. Spiritbox
Tsunami Sea
(Pale Chord Music / Rise Records)

Tsunami Sea is Spiritbox's most honest output thus far, told through a watery lens that's fixed on southern Vancouver Island and the Salish Sea. Vocalist Courtney Laplante describes quintessential West Coast images: islands disappearing in an oceanic fog, bleached animal bones littering rocky beaches and crystalline roses fused to the salt walls of cool seaside caves; the 808-style instrumentation throughout matches the crash of an ocean wave against jagged rock. With songs like "Keep Sweet," LaPlante has succeeded in dissolving the spines of metal bros who have defined the genre for far too long. It's Spiritbox's time, and this brave record lays bare all their insecurities, fears, pain and rage.
Em Medland-Marchen

34. Leanne Betasamosake Simpson
Live Like the Sky
(You've Changed Records)

Leanne Betasamosake Simpson taps into the punk and new wave music of her youth. While reliving the past, she remains in communion with the present, drawing musical influence from other contemporary Anishinaabe artists like shoegazing rockers OMBIIGIZI. When feeling unmoored, reconnecting with formative experiences can help a person course correct. The album's references to blue skies, shorelines and horizons are potent reminders of life's expansiveness in a world that aims to silo us and weaponize our isolation to fuel fascism. Remember to look up when fighting back.
Leslie Ken Chu

33. Freddie Gibbs and the Alchemist
Alfredo 2
(ESGN Records / ALC Records / Virgin Music Group)

Mercilessness never sounded so peerless, as Freddie Gibbs neutralizes a feud with the formidable Benny the Butcher on "Empanadas." A highlight from this latest team-up LP with perpetual-motion-prolific producer the Alchemist, the track has gradual looped strings that luxuriously cushion Gibbs shrugging off foes like Butcher. But the best revenge is living well, and he does just that across Alfredo 2. Gibbs chillingly compares his skills to the flame on a hate crime's cross ("Skinny Suge II"), and he spits vignette bars about the blood on his razor blade ("Lemon Pepper Steppers") and clearing a safe's cash ("Ensalada"), all with python-writhing flows.
Kyle Mullin

32. Nourished by Time 
The Passionate Ones
(XL Recordings)

Marcus Brown's weary voice belies his passionate conviction. It's what's carried Nourished by Time from his parents' Baltimore basement to international tours and recording studios, where he recorded his debut full-length. On it, he makes what was previously implicit explicit as he ruminates on the struggle for greatness. Ultimately, he leans hard into the idea that it only comes when you fully believe in what you're doing, be it in life, love or art. 
Ian Gormely

31. Deftones
private music
(Reprise Records / Warner Records)

Whoever said veteran bands can't make consistently strong, boundary-pushing music has never listened to Deftones. The Sacramento alt-metal legends' 10th album is a sterling addition to their catalogue, one blending hard rock, metal and shoegaze with razor-sharp precision and with frontman Chino Moreno in vintage form. Their range is on full display, jumping from aggressive ("my mind is a mountain") to melodic ("infinite source") to dreamy ("souvenir") to brooding ("departing the body"), or a combo of any of those.
Dave MacIntyre

30. Lambrini Girls
Who Let the Dogs Out
(City Slang)

In the year 2025, while women's rights are tactically eroded across the planet, Lambrini Girls couldn't have picked a more fitting time to drop their debut album — a collection of punk rock anthems flipping the bird primarily at capitalism and men (boooooooo). Its 30 minutes cover contemporary and institutional woes, from gentrification and workplace harassment to Kate Moss and nepo-babies, alongside a healthy sprinkle of queer love to balance out the dread. Chant-along lyrics fearlessly and concisely skewer the zeitgeist while Lambrini Girls' punk packaging (à la Amyl and the Sniffers or Peaches) offers cunty catharsis in the pit. 
Allie Gregory

29. Earl Sweatshirt
Live Laugh Love
(Tan Cressida Records / Warner Records)

Earl Sweatshirt continues to prove that his pen belongs in the pantheon of hip-hop's greatest. With Live Laugh Love, the now-31-year-old rapper's outlook has shifted toward an unbridled optimism for what lies ahead. Seemingly every verse contains poetic passages of wisdom from lessons past, as he finds peace in the process. It's an unapologetically warm and hopeful record that perfectly encapsulates Earl's mastery of his craft.
Wesley McLean

28. FKA twigs
EUSEXUA
(Young / Atlantic Records)

Refusing to be pigeonholed, UK singer-songwriter and producer FKA twigs delivers a daring evolution of dance music on EUSEXUA. Channelling her deep love for underground club culture, she crafts a sonic landscape where techno, house, raw rhythms and intimate vocals collide with powerful lyricism. EUSEXUA isn't just a bold highlight in a sea of dance tracks this year, but a statement about where the genre should be headed.
Vernon Ayiku

27. Bad Bunny
DeBÍ TiRAR MáS FOToS
(Rimas Entertainment)

Nostalgic, introspective, political yet heavy with club bangers, Bad Bunny's sixth album has catapulted the "King of Latin Trap" to a higher status than the musician has seen before. On "LO QUE LE PASÓ A HAWAii," Benito expresses the collective frustrations of Puerto Ricans with their continued colonization, resulting in massive TikTok popularity as communities with shared histories utilized the Spanish-language track to reflect their struggles. Soaked in pride for his island home, DeBÍ TiRAR MáS FOToS is an instant career highlight.
Sarah Jessica Rintjema

26. Emma Goldman
all you are is we
(Zegema Beach)

Launching the speculative anarchism of Ursula K. Le Guin's The Dispossessed into a blistering 26-minute collage of skramz, breakbeat, gabber and spoken word poetry, the debut from Vancouver screamo punks Emma Goldman delivers a modernist manifesto to detonate the dopamine-dosing alienation of our late capitalist milieu. Its imperfect disjoints rubbing up against each other with seismic results, all you are is we constructs an ideological bridge to a future of valued difference and shared emancipatory becoming.
Tom Beedham

25. Blood Orange
Essex Honey
(RCA Records)

Blood Orange's Essex Honey saw the multi-hyphenate artist share his most personal thoughts to date. An homage to his hometown, the record embodies the feeling of longing for the place you once called home and the nuances that come with such reckonings. Lined with grief and memory, adorned by a chorus of seasoned collaborators and immersive yet abstract instrumentation, Essex Honey gives us a front row seat into his past and present, while propelling us into his future.
Olive Soki-Kavwahirehi

24. Lorde
Virgin
(Universal Music Group)

With Virgin, Lorde has returned to the brooding synthpop that we can call her signature sound after a brief sojourn in sunny, beachside pop on Solar Power. While Virgin didn't quite reach the same critical and commercial heights as Melodrama, it shares plenty of DNA with the New Zealand artist's opus. What Virgin really represents is an artist making a course correction while still pushing forward. Virgin contains a trove of bold choices, and the maturity, vulnerability and depth of expression throughout these 11 tracks suggest that, as Lorde stands on the cusp of her 30s, she may be just scratching the surface of her artistry.
Adam Feibel

23. Perfume Genius
Glory
(Matador Records)

On his seventh studio LP as Perfume Genius, Michael Hadreas continues to solidify himself as one of the most forward-thinking acts in rock and pop. Taking a step back to move forward, this record relishes in the simplicity of its sound, while still feeling like it's pushing the boundaries of what he can do. Hadreas maximizes the crisp, lush instrumentation throughout the tracklist with palpably cathartic writing and performances that have become his trademark. 
Wesley McLean

22. Water from Your Eyes
It's a Beautiful Place
(Matador Records)

This album makes me feel like an iPad baby (complimentary). Nate Amos and Rachel Brown's textured bursts of jankpop colour have an addictive quality, growing on you each time "Playing Classics" threatens to detonate someone's shitty laptop speakers or the siren song "Nights in Armor" riff lures you into the deep. In the world of It's a Beautiful Place, being overwhelmed is embraced — and, with their respective blasé speak-singing and swathes of fuzz, the duo buffer the sharp edges of their own design.
Megan LaPierre

21. Agriculture
The Spiritual Sound
(The Flenser)

Agriculture's dizzying second full-length is the apex of "ecstatic black metal," the genre concocted by the band that best describes their euphoric noise. Exhilarating in both its quietest and its most harrowing moments, the eclectic album expands their sound and scope to something bordering on transcendental. Big riffs and screams sit side-by-side with ambience and warbling folk, the band influenced by everything from Lou Reed and Chan Buddhism to post-hardcore, Zen mysticism and, of course, Dylan. There's plenty of devastation, but there's hope in the ashes. \m/, indeed.
Marko Djurdjić

20. Ninajirachi
I Love My Computer
(NLV Records)

Like Jirachi, the mythical, wish-granting Pokémon of her mashup artist name, Ninajirachi, Nina Wilson works her own kind of magic. With her stunning debut LP, I Love My Computer, the Australian producer stakes her claim as electronic music's next big thing. Supercharged and sugary on the surface, I Love My Computer reveals personal reflections on growing up immersed in the internet. It's a refreshing and nostalgic celebration honouring the effervescent 2010s EDM that raised her, a versatile, kinetic and kaleidoscopic blend of dubstep, trance, hyperpop and beyond that flows like a fun, feeling-filled DJ set.
Chris Bryson

19. Alex G
Headlights
(RCA)

For all the possible pressure involved with making a major label debut after more than a decade of artistic independence, Alex G keeps a steady hand behind the wheel on Headlights. In the high beams, Alex Giannascoli's latest songs glint with greater polish than longtime passengers have known, and, despite new trappings like string sections and alleged wads of label cash, it's not an unfamiliar trip as pan flute synths and pitch-shifted vocals dot the landscape.
Calum Slingerland

18. Men I Trust
Equus Caballus
(Independent)

Montreal playlist conquerors Men I Trust divided their 2025 output into two equine albums: the mellow, folk-tinged Equus Asinus, followed by the more peppy, pop-oriented Equus Caballus. Which is to say, they saved all of the best shit for the latter album, a front-to-back collection of hooks and pure sonic sunshine, as the trio gallop through a fantastical land of soft-focus synths and wobbly new wave grooves delivered with the energy of showjumping and the precision of dressage.
Alex Hudson

17. Oklou
choke enough
(True Panther Sounds)

A twilit amalgam of Y2K fluorescence and subtle medieval flourishes, choke enough is one of 2025's weirder pop success stories. The often-drumless, carefully reserved wash of uncanny-valley pop music is Marylou Mayniel's full-length debut as Oklou, and it feels like an arrival — the kind of album that spawns a whole new class of not-so-subtle imitators. On a record chock-full of references both ancient and painfully contemporary, Mayniel never tips into pastiche, successfully distorting the original DNA into something entirely new. 
Kaelen Bell

16. PUP
Who Will Look After the Dogs?
(Rise Records)

The fifth album from Toronto-based rockers PUP is rife with angst and self-loathing. Who Will Look After the Dogs? is an album that is as emotional as it is conducive to sweaty mosh pit sing-alongs — something they brought to Toronto on their summer-defining residency tour, and across the country on the year-capping World's Coolest Guys Canada Tour.
Francis Baptiste

15. Tyler, the Creator
DON'T TAP THE GLASS
(Columbia)

You know we're cooked when someone has to lay out the rules before an album, but DON'T TAP THE GLASS, the surprise album that bodies its predecessor with about half the runtime, benefits from putting listeners in their place. With a resurrected Sk8brd — a.k.a. Pharrell — in tow, alongside some of the most danceable tracks Tyler has ever produced, it's impossible to keep still across its slim 28 minutes. And remember: don't tap the glass.
Nathan Chizen

14. Wet Leg
moisturizer
(Domino Recording Company)

Wet Leg arise from the chaise with confidence, expanding their sound without losing their wit. Between albums, vocalist Rhian Teasdale came out as queer, and she and guitarist Hester Chambers grew their duo to a five-piece, with touring bandmates Ellis Durand, Henry Holmes and Josh Mobaraki becoming creative contributors. The result highlights the band's existing strengths while establishing new ones; from the wry kiss-off of "mangetout" through the heartswell chorus of "pokemon" to victorious closer "u and me at home," moisturizer lets Wet Leg chart an unfolding sense of what they're capable of.
Paul Blinov

13. Dijon
Baby
(R&R / Warner Records)

While hardcore music lovers often foam at the mouth at the word "experimental," casual listeners — those just looking for something to soundtrack a commute, workout, party or late-night wind-down — can find the label alienating. Enter Dijon, the 33-year-old singer-songwriter-producer based in Los Angeles, widely known for his work on Justin Bieber's "Daisies" and "Yukon." On his sophomore album Baby, Dijon has earned near-universal praise for reshaping the boundaries of R&B, blending raw, unfiltered vocals with folk-tinged soundscapes and indie textures. Baby is a restless, emotionally rich, yet sonically minimalist alt-R&B record exploring themes of fatherhood and identity. It's approachable for casual and traditional R&B fans alike, but still boundary-pushing for those looking to expand their sonic palette. Most of all, it's deeply moving in its lyricism.
Vernon Ayiku

12. Olivia Dean
The Art of Loving
(Capitol)

Whether you wanted to or not, you probably heard an Olivia Dean song this year. Bubbly tracks like "Nice to Each Other" and "Man I Need" from the British singer-songwriter's second record The Art of Loving became massive hits that were used in what felt like every video posted to TikTok and Instagram. Thankfully, the algorithm never dulled Dean's warm retro pop sound, making it easy to fall for The Art of Loving again and again.
Laura Stanley

11. Clipse
Let God Sort Em Out
(ROC Nation Distribution)

In a year in which the Billboard Top 40 recently went without a single hip-hop song for the first time in 35 years, Clipse returned to the corner that they commanded so well in the early 2000s to remind everyone that the genre's pulse never waned — a couple of its rightful stewards were just missing. It's 2025, and the lines of coke that have always lain neatly between verses in Numbers and Leviticus feel just as potent as they did 15 years ago. But Let God Sort Em Out isn't a nostalgia grab; it's a feat of restoration. They sound touched by time, yes, but not in a way that paints them as a couple of old heads. Let God Sort Em Out renders the mainstream of hip-hop necessary, dire and grimy again. In the freshest of ways, Pusha T and Malice reemerged to help define the playing field again, and we're all better because of it.
Kyle Kohner

10. Slash Need
SIT & GRIN
(Independent)

Slash Need have distilled the gloriously chaotic, artful energy of their live show into one of the most electrifying debut albums in recent memory. The Toronto duo (and Exclaim! Class of 2023 alumni) welcome you into their world with inventive and immersive arrangements that pulse with driving beats and ooze with synth-punk goodness. The eight tracks are crafted for maximum impact, whether they are exploring the oppressive, stifling feeling of living in a small town on "BORDER TOWN" or celebrating the hunger of desire on "LEATHER." SIT & GRIN proves that catharsis is best experienced on the dance floor and in the mosh pit, preferably while wearing latex and ripped fishnets. 
Em Moore

9. Rosalía
LUX
(Columbia Records)

On LUX, Rosalía fuses classical grandeur with modern pop precision, expanding the emotional and sonic scope of her artistry. Where 2022's MOTOMAMI was brash and electrifying, LUX is cinematic and deeply felt. With an album this expansive and richly arranged, lesser artists might disappear in the mix, but her voice remains the central force of the record. Her vivid timbre glides between more than a dozen languages, shifting so seamlessly you hardly notice the transitions. By blending symphonic flair with electronic distortion, the album crafts a sound that defies genre conventions, standing entirely on its own. Rosalía forges a modern gospel, full of melodrama, unabashed heartbreak, and an ode to divine womanhood.
Kieran Lauzier

8. Deafheaven
Lonely People with Power
(Roadrunner Records)

Fifteen years into their career as fierce genre innovators, Deafheaven's colossal sixth LP stands as an enduring testament to formal synthesis. On Lonely People with Power, vocalist and lyricist George Clarke harnesses the San Francisco quintet's intricate fusion of molten blackgaze ("Magnolia," "Doberman"), dark pop ("Heathen"), post-punk groove ("Body Behavior") and foreboding darkwave ("Incidental II") to deliver an arresting meditation on 2025's overall vibe, namely how interpersonal shortcomings and patriarchal perspectives continue to shape and distort our worldview in ways both intentional and destructive.
Owen Morawitz

7. Hayley Williams
Ego Death at a Bachelorette Party
(Post Atlantic)

Hayley Williams continues to reach new heights. While she has led Paramore's pop-rock reign since their 2005 debut, Williams's artistry elevates on her third solo project. Her best solo work by far, Williams offers sharp, diaristic songwriting that laments love, loneliness and trauma. While 20 songs may appear excessive, each one earns its spot on the tracklist, as Williams transparently unpacks the chaos inside her brain. Her authenticity seeps into Ego Death's DIY rollout — from surprise-dropping 17 singles via her Y2K-style website to gradually releasing the project's final three tracks to a thoughtful talk show performance of "True Believer." Marking the first record on her indie label Post Atlantic — a cheeky reference to Williams's departure from Atlantic Records — Ego is an artistic rebirth, with Williams doing things on her terms.
Heather Taylor-Singh

6. Wednesday
Bleeds
(Dead Oceans)

Described by vocalist Karly Hartzman as the "spiritual successor" to 2023's Rat Saw God, Wednesday find themselves on year-end lists once again with the maelstrom Bleeds. Hartzman expertly spins webs — both personal and anecdotal — of blistering, country-infused rock as the North Carolina quintet peel back layers of flesh to reveal the muscles underneath, pulsing and alive. Bleeds is not a violent affair, however; the shimmering "Elderberry Wine" and achingly delicate "The Way Love Goes" showcase Wednesday's ability to pull themselves back right before careening over the edge. An unstoppable force, Bleeds sinks its teeth into you and won't let go.
Karlie Rogers

5. Saya Gray
SAYA
(Dirty Hit)

Undeniable skill and indelible idiosyncrasy define the confident growth Toronto's Saya Gray has demonstrated with one of 2025's most unforgettable musical statements. Gray is firmly positioned in the tradition of extremely talented and technically adept musical artists who marry ability and historical acumen with unbridled creativity. SAYA blends myriad generations and styles of songwriting, referencing decades of rock, pop, folk, country and soul music, and filtering it all through a lens of modern experimental production techniques and inventive arrangements. All of that would be impressive sonic dressing on its own, but, at its core, SAYA is built on fantastic, timeless songwriting with melodies and lyrics that reach directly into your soul and stir emotions that can only be articulated through the magical communication of music.
Scott A. Gray

4. Turnstile
NEVER ENOUGH
(Roadrunner Records)

The kids are alright when they're listening to the latest record from Baltimore's Turnstile. In a world increasingly saturated with AI slop, there's something powerful in being so authentically human that can make a record stand above the fray — especially when experienced with a live crowd. Expanding on the evolution of their sound from 2021's GLOW ON, Turnstile don't shy away from showing their fans how different scenes and styles can show up in their hardcore world, like how "LOOK OUT FOR ME" winds down into a B'more club interlude.
Vanessa Tam

3. PinkPantheress
Fancy That
(Warner Records)

This year, we listened to PinkPantheress introduce herself and tell us how glad she was to meet us countless times as we idly scrolled through TikTok and Instagram, but her cheeky, shimmery pop greeting never lost its charm. Making an impact in a short time is nothing new for PinkPantheress; at a brisk 20 minutes, Fancy That is the singer-producer's most fully realized and infectious project to date. Layers of kinetic UK dance pop, garage and electronica bubble and erupt with personality, and her signature power of transforming nostalgic samples into completely new beasts has never sounded cleaner. Each replay unveils a new hidden gem, making Fancy That the hazy club set you could stay up and dance to forever.
Jordan Currie

2. Geese
Getting Killed
(Partisan Records / Play It Again Sam)

Getting Killed helped Brooklyn's Geese break through into the broader culture — no small feat in an era in which rock is more of a pose than a sound. What's even more notable is that they did it with such an uncompromising record. Where the band had previously played with the concept of genre, here they subsume it, put it through its paces, and spit out something new. Singer Cameron Winter, hot off a pretty great solo record, truly comes into his own as a frontman. He used to sing soul music; now his singing is soulful, the crux of songs whose bones feel eerily familiar, even if their roots can't be readily identified. It flies in the face of an algorithm-driven culture that rewards the familiar, making a record that would stand out in any year an all the more remarkable achievement. 
Ian Gormely

1. Ribbon Skirt
Bite Down
(Mint Records)

Too often, an album comes out swinging so hard at the start that you inevitably overlook the late-LP filler. Ribbon Skirt, on the other hand, are inventive and self-assured from Bite Down's first seconds to its last. Featuring vocalist-guitarist Tashiina Buswa and multi-instrumentalist Billy Riley, formerly half of Montreal indie rockers Love Language, the duo pack their debut with instinct and swagger. It's the kind of short, bracing piece of art that disarms listeners with bursts of clarity. Lyrics like "I want to preserve every part that makes me" ("Off Rez") and "It's getting harder not to feel so abandoned" ("Wrong Planet") take on colonization, identity and intergenerational trauma head-on, adding to the urgency of the record. On Bite Down, Ribbon Skirt proudly carry the flag for brash and hook-laden Mint predecessors like the New Pornographers and the Organ. If you're wondering what Canadian artists do better than anyone else in the world, it's exactly this.
Daniel Sylvester
